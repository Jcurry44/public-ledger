Niagara County’s money

Niagara County took in $569.1M and spent $557.4M in 2025, 3.3% more spending than 2024. 31 years of its own annual reports to the state, 1995–2025.

Towns & cities: all twenty, on the map and ranked→

What these numbers are

Self-reported by the county to the NYS Comptroller, which reviews the filings at a desk but does not audit them. Figures are as filed, not adjusted for inflation. The county does not publish an itemized claims register on its own site: Legislature agendas and minutes carry resolutions only (the numbered items its members vote on); one county sewer district’s board does list vendor vouchers, in its own minutes (checked Oct 5, 2026, source). The city ledger has a register because North Tonawanda posts its warrants.

Adopted budget book · 2025

Budget vs actual: what was planned, and what was spent

What the Legislature adopted for 2025, next to what the county’s 2025 filing says actually happened, line by line, from the county’s adopted budget book. The filing includes every change made during the year, so each difference is measured against the original promise.

What is an appropriation?

An appropriation is a spending limit: the amount the Legislature adopts for a budget line, the most the county has been allowed to spend on it for the year. The table sets each adopted limit (“budgeted”) beside what the county’s filing says was spent (“actual”): under means spending came in below the limit, over means it ended above the original one.

Every resolution behind these dollars

The county can’t spend, contract, settle, or move budget money without its Legislature voting on a numbered resolution. This ledger shows where the money went; Every resolution is the record of who voted to send it.

What are a resolution and a ceiling?

A resolution is a numbered item on the Legislature’s agenda. Members vote on it at a meeting, and the minutes record the vote. The county needs one to spend, contract, settle or move budget money.

A ceiling is a spending limit: the most one resolution lets the county spend on something — permission to spend up to that figure, not a payment. Ceilings are never added together on this page.

How it was built

Built the same way as the city ledger. The Comptroller publishes every county’s accounts as one statewide file; this page takes Niagara County’s 31 years of account-level rows (16,772 of them), matched by the state’s own code for the county, money in and money out only (balance-sheet rows are left out). The state changed its reporting format in 2013; every chart marks the change. If a year goes missing or a total looks wrong, the page does not update.
