Warrant? Control total? What the words mean

A warrant is the City’s list of the claims the Common Council approves for payment at a meeting, usually twice a month: each claim a payee, an amount and the city account it is charged to. Approval is not payment; a claim here does not show that a check cleared. A card statement is the same list for the city’s purchasing cards.

A control total is the total the city prints for each fund on a warrant. The claims charged to that fund should add up to it to the penny, and this site checks that they do. A few claims sit outside the funds’ controls (trust and general-ledger claims, or revenue-type claims); with them, the claims add up to the list total.

OCR (optical character recognition) is software that reads the letters in a picture. When the city posts a warrant only as a scanned image, its claims are read this way, and the warrant is used only when every total printed on it ties to the penny.

Flag 03

Accounts far off their own thirty-year history

Which of these are one-time events and which are the new normal? Capital-project accounts are left out: they are one-time by design.

How an account gets on this list

Each account below is at least 3× its own prior-year median and at least $50K. The median uses the account’s own earlier filed years (at least five; absent or zero years skipped), as filed, across the 2013 basis change. A match is a reason to ask, not a measure of error. Capital-fund accounts (H-prefix) are left out: they are one-time by design, and the capital projects section of the ledger covers them. Every account over the threshold is listed — nothing is truncated. The sparkline answers the question the table cannot: a spike, or a climb.

How this page was built

66 flags, generated automatically. From 52 warrants + 30 years of filings, with warrants through Oct 20, 2026 (the date printed on the newest warrant). Every flag was computed from the public record — no judgment calls, no tips, no access to anything the city has not published. Each one is a question, not a finding: the likely explanation for most is routine, and several have answers a treasurer could give in a sentence. That is the point — these are the questions the data says to ask first.

Method. Generated by Public Ledger from the City of North Tonawanda’s published Warrant of Claims (52 parsed warrants, reconciled exactly to the control totals printed on each document) and its annual filings to the NYS Comptroller (1995–2024, self-reported). Thresholds: 3× an account’s own prior-year median and $50K minimum; first appearances in this register of $100K or more, measured only where at least 10 earlier warrants exist. Sparklines run from zero to each account’s own high; a year the account is absent from the filing is left blank, not drawn as zero; the state’s reporting basis changed in 2013 and is marked, not smoothed. “Worth asking first” ranks by dollar size alone and is not a total. Approvals and filings as published, not audited actuals. Prepared independently from public records; not requested by or produced for the City.
